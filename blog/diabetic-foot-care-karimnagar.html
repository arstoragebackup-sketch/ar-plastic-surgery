Diabetic Foot Care in Karimnagar: A Complete Guide

Short answer: Diabetes damages the nerves and blood vessels in the feet, so small injuries can go unnoticed and turn into ulcers that heal slowly. Diabetic foot care means daily foot checks, prompt treatment of any break in the skin, pressure relief, good blood-sugar control, and specialist assessment when a wound does not improve. This guide explains why diabetic feet are at risk, the warning signs to act on, how ulcers are treated, and when surgery or reconstruction is needed.

Why diabetes harms the feet

Two problems combine. Nerve damage (neuropathy) reduces feeling, so a blister, cut or stone in a shoe may go unnoticed. Poor circulation means less oxygen and fewer healing cells reach the wound, so it closes slowly and infection takes hold more easily.

Pressure adds a third problem. When feeling is reduced, a person keeps walking on an injured spot, and the same pressure that caused the sore keeps it open. Foot shape changes over time can create new pressure points under the toes or the ball of the foot.

  • Numbness, tingling or burning in the feet
  • Loss of feeling, so injuries go unnoticed
  • Dry, cracked skin that splits easily
  • Foot shape changes or new pressure points
  • Slow healing of even small cuts

What is a diabetic foot ulcer?

A diabetic foot ulcer is an open sore on the foot of a person with diabetes, usually on the sole, heel or toes, where pressure and reduced feeling meet. Some ulcers are shallow and heal with wound care and pressure relief; deeper ones can reach tendon or bone and become infected.

Ulcers often start from something small: a tight shoe, a blister, a cut while trimming nails, a burn from hot water, or walking barefoot. Because pain may be absent, the first sign is sometimes a stain on a sock, a smell, or swelling — not pain.

Warning signs: act the same day

  • Any cut, blister or sore on the foot that does not start improving within a day or two
  • Redness, warmth or swelling around a wound
  • Pus, bad smell or discharge
  • Black or discoloured skin on a toe or the foot
  • New swelling of one foot or ankle
  • Fever with a foot wound
  • A foot that suddenly changes shape, or a toe that looks displaced

Important: a spreading red area, fever, blackened skin, or a wound that reaches deep tissue needs urgent medical care the same day — do not wait for a routine appointment.

Daily foot care that prevents ulcers

  • Check both feet every day, including the soles — use a mirror or ask a family member if bending is hard
  • Wash with lukewarm water (test with your elbow, not your foot) and dry well, especially between the toes
  • Moisturise the feet but not between the toes
  • Cut nails straight across and file sharp edges; do not cut cuticles or dig at corners
  • Never walk barefoot, indoors or outdoors
  • Wear well-fitting shoes and break new shoes in slowly; shake shoes out before putting them on
  • Keep blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol as close to your targets as your doctor advises, and do not smoke — smoking narrows the blood vessels that heal wounds

How a diabetic foot ulcer is assessed

Assessment starts with the wound itself — its size, depth, discharge and smell — and the foot around it: feeling, pulses, circulation, pressure points and footwear. Blood tests, wound swabs if infection is suspected, X-rays if bone involvement is possible, and circulation tests help complete the picture.

The team also reviews diabetes control, medicines, kidney and heart health, and nutrition, because all of these affect healing. Treatment is planned around the wound and the person together, not the wound alone.

Treatment: wound care and offloading

The first steps are cleaning the wound, removing dead tissue (debridement), treating infection if present, and choosing a dressing that keeps the wound moist but not wet. Equally important is offloading — taking pressure off the ulcer with special footwear, a cast or a removable walker, so it can close.

Many ulcers heal with this combination plus diabetes and circulation care. Progress is checked regularly; a wound that is not getting smaller over a few weeks needs its plan changed, not simply more of the same dressing.

When surgery or reconstruction is needed

Surgery is needed when a wound will not close, when infection reaches deeper tissue or bone, when dead tissue must be removed, or when a pressure point or deformity keeps reopening the same spot. Options depend on the case: debridement, skin grafting for a clean wound bed, or flap surgery — moving healthy tissue with its own blood supply — to cover a deeper defect.

In some cases, correcting a toe deformity or removing a prominent bone reduces pressure and stops the ulcer returning. Amputation is a last resort, used when tissue cannot be saved or infection threatens life or limb; early specialist care is what keeps that option rare.

Aftercare and preventing recurrence

A healed ulcer is still a high-risk foot. The same pressure point, the same shoes and the same reduced feeling remain, so recurrence is common without follow-up. Therapeutic footwear, regular foot checks, prompt care of any new break in the skin, and continued diabetes care are the long-term plan.

Keep every follow-up appointment even after the wound closes, and seek care the same day for any new sore — early treatment is far simpler than treating an established ulcer.

Common questions

What is a diabetic foot ulcer?

An open sore on the foot of a person with diabetes, usually where pressure and reduced feeling meet — most often the sole, heel or toes. Small injuries can become ulcers because pain may be absent and healing is slow.

Why do diabetic foot wounds heal slowly?

Nerve damage removes the pain warning, poor circulation delivers less oxygen to the wound, and continued pressure on the spot keeps reopening it. All three must be addressed for healing.

When should I see a doctor urgently?

The same day for spreading redness, fever, pus or bad smell, blackened skin, a deep wound, or a foot that suddenly swells or changes shape. These can signal infection or tissue damage that worsens quickly.

Can a diabetic foot ulcer heal without surgery?

Many do, with wound care, infection treatment, pressure relief (offloading) and diabetes care. Surgery is needed when a wound will not close, infection reaches deeper tissue, or a deformity keeps reopening the same spot.

Will I lose my foot if I have an ulcer?

Not usually. Most ulcers heal with timely care. Amputation is a last resort for tissue that cannot be saved or infection that threatens life or limb — early specialist care is what keeps that risk low.

How can I prevent another ulcer?

Daily foot checks, never walking barefoot, well-fitting shoes, good diabetes control, not smoking, and same-day care for any new cut or blister — plus follow-up even after the wound closes.
