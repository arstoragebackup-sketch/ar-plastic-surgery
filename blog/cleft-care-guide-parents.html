Cleft Lip and Palate: A Compassionate Guide for Parents

Learning your baby has a cleft lip or palate can feel overwhelming. But this is a well-understood condition with a clear path forward, and you will not walk it alone.

By Dr Ashok Reddy, MBBS, DNB (Mumbai), M.Ch (Delhi), Gold Medalist — Plastic, Cosmetic, Hand & Microvascular Surgeon

First, Breathe

A cleft happens when the lip or the roof of the mouth does not fully join during early pregnancy. It is one of the more common birth differences, it is nobody's fault, and nothing you did or did not do caused it. Hearing that clearly matters, because guilt is the heaviest thing many parents carry.

What follows is a journey, not an emergency. Cleft care is staged over months and years by a team, and children who receive it generally grow up eating, speaking and smiling like their peers. The path is well mapped; your job is simply to take it one step at a time.

Feeding Your Baby

Feeding is usually the first practical challenge, because a cleft palate makes suction difficult. Specialised bottles and teats, upright positioning and guidance from your care team help most babies feed well and gain weight normally.

If feeding directly is difficult, expressed milk still gives your baby its benefits. Your team will monitor weight gain closely in the early weeks; good nutrition also prepares your baby for surgery when the time comes.

The Staged Journey Ahead

Cleft treatment is deliberately staged. Lip repair is generally planned in the early months of life, and palate repair follows later in infancy; further refinements to the nose, speech or jaw may be scheduled through childhood as growth dictates.

Staging is not delay, it is design. Each step is timed to growth and development so that function and appearance build together. Your surgeon will explain the sequence planned for your child and why each stage sits where it does.

Speech, Hearing and Teeth

Because the palate shapes speech, children are monitored for speech development, and speech therapy is part of care for many. Hearing checks matter too, since fluid behind the eardrum is common with cleft palate and is easily managed when caught early.

Dental care runs alongside: clefts can affect tooth development and alignment, so regular dental review from an early age keeps small issues from becoming large ones. The team approach, surgeon, speech therapist, dentist and audiologist, exists so nothing falls through the cracks.

Caring for the Whole Child, and Yourself

Your child will take emotional cues from you. Matter-of-fact warmth about the cleft, honest age-appropriate answers to questions, and celebrating every milestone build the confidence that carries children through stares and questions from others.

And care for yourself too. Connecting with other cleft families, asking your team anything without embarrassment, and acknowledging that some days are hard, all of this is part of good parenting, not a deviation from it. You are doing better than you think.

FAQ

Common questions

What causes a cleft lip or palate?

A cleft occurs when facial structures do not fully join during early pregnancy. It results from a mix of genetic and environmental factors, is nobody's fault, and is one of the more common birth differences seen worldwide.

Will my child need multiple surgeries?

Usually, yes, care is staged. Lip and palate repairs happen in infancy, with possible later refinements for the nose, speech or jaw as your child grows. Your surgeon will outline the sequence planned for your child.

Will my child speak normally?

With timely palate repair and speech therapy where needed, most children develop clear, intelligible speech. Regular speech assessment through childhood catches any issues early, when they are simplest to address.

When should we first see a specialist?

As early as possible, ideally in the first weeks of life. Early assessment guides feeding, plans the surgical sequence and connects you with the full care team from the start.
