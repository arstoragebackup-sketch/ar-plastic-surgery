When a Wound Won't Heal: Causes and When to See a Plastic Surgeon in Karimnagar

Short answer: most everyday cuts and surgical wounds close within two to three weeks. A wound that is still open after four weeks, keeps reopening, or is getting larger instead of smaller is called a non-healing or chronic wound — and it is a sign that something is getting in the way of healing, not a failure of dressings alone. This guide explains what counts as non-healing, the common causes, the red flags that need same-day care, and how a plastic surgeon assesses and treats wounds that will not close.

What counts as a non-healing wound?

Healing normally moves through stages: bleeding stops, inflammation clears debris, new tissue fills the gap, and the surface closes. A wound is considered slow or non-healing when it shows little or no progress after about four weeks of proper care, or when it closes and then breaks open again in the same spot.

Non-healing wounds are not one condition. They include diabetic foot ulcers, pressure sores, leg ulcers from poor vein or artery circulation, wounds after injury or surgery that have come apart, burns that will not epithelialise, and wounds kept open by infection or a foreign body.

Common causes: why healing stalls

  • Poor blood supply — diabetes, smoking, or narrowed arteries starve the wound of oxygen
  • Pressure or repeated trauma — lying, sitting or walking on the same spot reopens it daily
  • Infection — bacteria, including biofilm on the wound surface, keep inflammation going
  • Dead tissue — slough or dead skin blocks new tissue from forming
  • Swelling (oedema) — fluid in the leg prevents oxygen reaching the skin
  • Uncontrolled diabetes or poor nutrition — high blood sugar and low protein slow every stage of healing
  • Medicines — steroids, some immune-suppressing drugs and blood thinners can delay closure
  • Foreign material — a stitch, splinter or exposed tendon or bone keeps the wound open

Often more than one cause is present. That is why changing the dressing without finding the cause rarely works — the cause must be treated alongside the wound.

Red flags: seek care the same day

  • Spreading redness, increasing pain, warmth or swelling around the wound
  • Fever, chills or feeling generally unwell
  • Pus, a bad smell, or rapidly increasing discharge
  • Black, grey or white tissue in the wound
  • A wound that suddenly deepens, or exposes tendon, bone or an implant
  • Bleeding that will not stop with pressure
  • A wound after surgery that opens up, or leaks fluid

Important: fever with a wound, blackened tissue, or a wound that reaches deep structures needs urgent medical care the same day.

What you can do at home — and what not to do

  • Keep the wound clean and covered with a clean dressing; change it as advised, not more often than needed
  • Do not apply antiseptic creams, turmeric, toothpaste or home remedies inside an open wound — they damage new tissue
  • Do not soak a chronic wound in water for long periods
  • Take pressure off the area: offload a foot ulcer, reposition regularly for a pressure sore, elevate a swollen leg
  • Eat adequately — healing needs protein, and uncontrolled blood sugar needs attention
  • Do not smoke, and tell your doctor about all medicines, including steroids

Home care supports healing; it does not replace assessment when a wound has stalled. If there is no visible improvement within two weeks of proper home care, get it assessed.

How a plastic surgeon assesses a non-healing wound

Assessment starts with the wound — size, depth, tissue type, discharge, odour and surrounding skin — and moves outward: blood supply (pulses, circulation tests), feeling, pressure points, footwear or seating, diabetes control, nutrition, medicines and previous treatments. Swabs, blood tests, X-rays or scans are used when infection, bone involvement or poor circulation is suspected.

The key question is not only 'what dressing?' but 'what is stopping this wound from healing?' — and the treatment plan answers both.

Treatment options

  • Wound-bed preparation — cleaning, debridement of dead tissue, and infection control, so healthy tissue can grow
  • Modern dressings — chosen for the wound's moisture level, not by habit: too dry or too wet both delay healing
  • Offloading and compression — pressure relief for foot and pressure wounds; compression for venous leg ulcers, once artery supply is confirmed adequate
  • Negative-pressure wound therapy — a sealed dressing with gentle suction, used for selected larger or deeper wounds
  • Skin grafting — thin skin taken from elsewhere on the body to close a clean, healthy wound bed
  • Flap surgery — moving healthy tissue with its own blood supply to cover a deeper defect, exposed tendon or bone, or a wound in an area that needs durable cover
  • Correcting the cause — improving circulation, diabetes control, pressure relief or deformity correction, without which even a graft will fail

Aftercare: keeping a healed wound closed

A healed chronic wound remains vulnerable. The same pressure, circulation problem or diabetes is still there, so recurrence is common. Protective footwear, pressure relief, skin care, continued diabetes and circulation care, and prompt attention to any new break in the skin are the long-term plan — plus follow-up even after closure.

Common questions

How long should a normal wound take to heal?

Most everyday cuts close within two to three weeks. A wound showing little or no progress after about four weeks of proper care is considered slow or non-healing and should be assessed.

Why won't my wound heal?

Usually because something blocks healing: poor blood supply, pressure on the wound, infection, dead tissue, swelling, uncontrolled diabetes, poor nutrition, certain medicines, or foreign material in the wound. Often more than one cause is present.

When should I see a plastic surgeon about a wound?

When a wound has not improved after two to four weeks of proper care, keeps reopening in the same spot, is deep, exposes tendon or bone, or follows surgery or a burn. Earlier is better — a stalled wound is easier to treat than an infected one.

What is the difference between a skin graft and a flap?

A skin graft is thin skin moved onto a clean, healthy wound bed, where it must grow a new blood supply. A flap moves tissue with its own blood supply attached, so it can cover deeper defects and exposed tendon or bone.

Can a non-healing wound become dangerous?

Yes, if infection spreads, tissue dies, or bone becomes involved. Fever, spreading redness, blackened tissue or a rapidly worsening wound need same-day medical care.

Will the wound come back after it heals?

It can, because the underlying cause — pressure, circulation, diabetes — often remains. Protective footwear, pressure relief, skin care and follow-up after closure are what keep it closed.
