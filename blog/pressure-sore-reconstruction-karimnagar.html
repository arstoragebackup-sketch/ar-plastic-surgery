Pressure Sore Reconstruction in Karimnagar: A Complete Guide

Short answer: A pressure sore — also called a bedsore or pressure injury — is damage to the skin and tissue underneath caused by sustained pressure over a bony area. Most early sores heal with pressure relief and good wound care. Deep, non-healing sores with exposed tissue need surgery: the dead tissue is removed and the wound is covered with healthy tissue that carries its own blood supply. This guide explains the stages, who is at risk, why some sores will not heal, prevention, when surgery is needed, and how recovery and recurrence prevention work.

What are pressure sores?

When a person lies or sits in one position for a long time, the weight of the body presses the skin and tissue against a bone. The pressure squeezes the small blood vessels shut, cutting off blood flow. Without blood, the tissue is starved of oxygen and nutrients, and it begins to break down. That breakdown is a pressure sore.

They form most often over bony prominences: the tailbone (sacrum), the sitting bones (ischium), the hips, the heels, the ankles, the shoulder blades and the back of the head. Because the damage starts deep and works outward, a sore can look small on the surface while being much deeper underneath.

Stages 1–4

  • Stage 1: The skin is intact but red or discoloured, and the area may feel warm, painful or different in firmness. The redness does not fade when pressure is removed. At this stage the damage is reversible with pressure relief.
  • Stage 2: The outer skin breaks — a shallow open sore, blister or abrasion. The wound is superficial but needs proper care to avoid deepening.
  • Stage 3: The sore extends through the skin into the fat layer beneath, forming a crater. Dead tissue may be visible, and infection risk rises.
  • Stage 4: The sore reaches muscle, tendon or bone. These are deep, often infected, and do not heal with dressings alone — this is where surgery is usually needed.

There are also sores whose depth cannot be judged because dead tissue covers the wound, and deep tissue injuries where the skin looks intact but the tissue underneath is damaged. A clinician grades the sore at examination.

Who is at risk?

  • Bedridden patients — those who cannot turn or reposition themselves, including after stroke, major surgery or prolonged illness.
  • Wheelchair users — constant sitting pressure on the sitting bones and tailbone.
  • Elderly people — thinner skin, less fat padding, and often reduced mobility.
  • People with spinal cord injury — loss of sensation means no pain signal to shift position, and loss of movement removes natural repositioning.
  • People with diabetes — poor circulation and nerve damage slow healing and blunt warning pain.
  • Poor circulation or poor nutrition — low protein intake, anaemia, dehydration and vitamin deficiency all weaken the skin and slow repair.
  • Incontinence and moisture — damp skin breaks down faster and is more prone to infection.

Why some sores do not heal

A sore will not heal while its cause is still acting. If pressure continues on the same spot, new damage outpaces repair. Other reasons include:

  • Dead tissue in the wound — it blocks healing and feeds infection.
  • Infection — including infection of the underlying bone, which dressings cannot reach.
  • Exposed muscle, tendon or bone — these tissues cannot grow new skin over themselves.
  • Poor blood supply — from diabetes, circulation problems or the pressure itself.
  • Poor nutrition — healing needs protein and calories; a malnourished patient heals slowly or not at all.
  • Shear and friction — sliding in bed or a chair tears fragile tissue and reopens healing wounds.

When a sore has been stuck for weeks despite proper dressings and pressure relief, it needs specialist assessment rather than more of the same dressing.

Prevention basics

  • Repositioning: change position at least every two hours in bed, and shift weight every 15–30 minutes in a wheelchair. A written turning schedule helps caregivers stay consistent.
  • Pressure relief: pressure-relieving mattresses, cushions and heel protectors spread the load. Check that equipment fits the patient and is actually in use.
  • Skin care: inspect the skin daily, especially over bony areas. Keep skin clean and dry, moisturise dry skin, and treat incontinence promptly.
  • Nutrition: adequate protein, calories and fluids. A dietitian's input helps when appetite is poor.
  • Movement: whatever movement is possible — assisted exercises, physiotherapy, or simply sitting out of bed — improves circulation.
  • Early action: redness that does not fade is a warning, not a waiting game. Relieve pressure on that spot immediately and seek advice if it worsens.

When surgery is needed

Surgery is considered when:

  • The sore is deep (stage 3 or 4) or its depth cannot be judged because of dead tissue.
  • It has not healed despite good wound care, pressure relief and nutrition.
  • Muscle, tendon or bone is exposed.
  • There is infection that does not settle, or infection of the underlying bone.
  • The wound keeps breaking open again after partial healing.

Before surgery, the patient is prepared: infection is treated, nutrition is improved, blood tests and anaemia are corrected, and a pressure-relief plan for after surgery is agreed. Surgery on a malnourished or infected patient heals poorly, so preparation is part of the treatment, not a delay.

What reconstruction involves

  • Debridement. All dead and infected tissue is removed until healthy, bleeding tissue is reached. This is essential — closing a wound over dead tissue guarantees failure.
  • Flap surgery. The usual reconstruction: nearby healthy skin, fat and sometimes muscle is moved over the wound, keeping its own blood vessels attached. Because the flap brings its own blood supply, it can cover bone and deep tissue and heal where a graft would fail. The flap is chosen for the site — for example, different flaps suit the tailbone, the sitting bones and the hip.
  • Skin grafting, where suitable. For shallower wounds with a healthy, well-supplied base, a thin layer of skin taken from another area can cover the wound. Grafts need a good bed to take, so they are not the answer for deep wounds over bone.
  • Drainage and closure. Drains may be left briefly to prevent fluid collecting under the flap. The wound is closed with care to avoid tension, and the donor area is closed directly or grafted.

Recovery and aftercare

  • Offloading is the key rule. After surgery, the operated area must be kept free of pressure — typically no sitting or lying on it for several weeks, as advised by the surgeon. This is the hardest part for patients and families, and it decides whether the flap survives.
  • Wound care: dressings as instructed, watching for redness, swelling, discharge or opening of the wound.
  • Nutrition continues: protein-rich diet and fluids support healing through the recovery weeks.
  • Gradual return to sitting: sitting is reintroduced step by step — short periods, building up, with a pressure-relieving cushion — only when the surgeon advises.
  • Follow-up visits: to check flap healing, remove stitches or drains, and adjust the sitting plan.
  • Physiotherapy: to maintain strength and mobility during the offloading period.

Recurrence prevention

Pressure sores can come back in the same place, because the risk factors — immobility, loss of sensation, sitting pressure — usually remain. Prevention after surgery is a long-term plan, made together by the surgeon, patient and family:

  • Keep the repositioning schedule going permanently, not just until the wound closes.
  • Use and maintain pressure-relieving cushions and mattresses; replace them when they wear out.
  • Check the skin daily and act on redness immediately.
  • Keep nutrition adequate and manage diabetes and circulation problems.
  • Attend follow-up even after healing — early review catches a new sore at stage 1, when it is still reversible.

Common questions

What is a pressure sore?

Damage to the skin and tissue underneath caused by sustained pressure over a bony area — most often the tailbone, hips, heels or shoulder blades — which cuts off blood flow and breaks the tissue down.

Who is most at risk?

Bedridden patients, wheelchair users, elderly people, people with spinal injury, and people with diabetes, poor circulation or poor nutrition. Reduced sensation is a major risk, because pain does not warn the person to shift position.

Why do some pressure sores not heal?

Because pressure keeps acting, and because deep sores involve dead tissue, infection, or exposed muscle or bone that cannot heal by dressings alone. Poor blood supply and poor nutrition also slow healing.

When is surgery needed?

When a sore is deep, does not heal despite good wound care and pressure relief, or has exposed muscle, tendon or bone. Surgery removes dead tissue and covers the wound with healthy tissue that has its own blood supply.

What is flap surgery?

Moving nearby healthy skin, fat and sometimes muscle — with its own blood vessels — over the wound. Because the flap brings its own blood supply, it can heal over bone or deep tissue where a skin graft alone would fail.

Can pressure sores come back after surgery?

Yes, if pressure returns to the same area. Regular repositioning, pressure-relieving equipment, skin care, nutrition and follow-up are as important as the surgery itself.
